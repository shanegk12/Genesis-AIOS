Part 2: The Importance of Recycling and Reuse

Once a product reaches its end-of-life, we have several choices for its components and materials: disposal, reuse, or recycling. In the Creationeering™ paradigm, the goal is to avoid waste and honor the resources God has given us. Recycling is converting an existing product or material into a new product. Reuse means using an item again, either for its original purpose or a new one, without significant changes. For example, about 12-15 million vehicles in the US alone reach their end-of-life each year. These vehicles are shredded, and metals, polymers, and glass are recovered for recycling. Currently, about 75% of vehicle materials can be recycled, with the remaining 25% ending up in landfills.

For your mousetrap car, consider the materials you've used. Can the wooden chassis be sanded down and used for another project? Can the plastic wheels be melted and remolded into something new? Can the metal axles be separated and sent to a metal recycling facility? Even the mousetrap itself could potentially be reused. Thinking about these options helps reduce waste and ensures that the valuable resources used in your car don't just disappear into a landfill.

Let's connect this to Multiscale Modeling. At a macro-level, you decide to recycle the aluminum can that served as your car's body. At the atomic level, recycling aluminum involves melting it down. During this process, the aluminum atoms rearrange themselves into a new structure, ready to form a new product. This is much more energy-efficient than extracting new aluminum from ore, which requires breaking down complex mineral compounds. Understanding this atomic-level process reinforces the value of recycling.

Using the OCV method for recycling: Objective: Maximize the percentage of your mousetrap car's materials that can be recycled or reused. Constraint: Limited to local recycling facilities' capabilities. Variable: Choosing between a wooden chassis (easily composted/repurposed) versus a plastic one (potentially recyclable if the right type of plastic).

Part 3: Designing for Disassembly and Circularity

True Junior Creationeers don't just design for performance; they design for the entire lifecycle, including the end. This means "designing for death and recycling." It involves making choices during the initial design phase that will make it easier to reuse or recycle materials later. For instance, if you use screws instead of glue, it's much easier to take components apart for reuse. If you use a single type of plastic for all plastic parts, it's easier to sort and recycle them together.

The Horstemeyer et al. paper mentions the concept of a "materials circulation system" where a product's secondary objective is to be reprocessed into a new product. This is about being creative and finding "new ways of deploying and putting them to use". This concept of circularity—keeping materials in use for as long as possible—is a cornerstone of sustainable engineering.

Think about your mousetrap car. Did you use different types of glue for different parts? This might make it harder to separate materials. Are your wheels permanently attached to the axles? Could they be designed to be easily removed? These considerations are part of designing for disassembly, which is crucial for effective recycling and reuse.

From a Multiscale Modeling perspective, if your car's chassis is made of wood, at the macro-level, it can be composted. At the molecular level, microorganisms break down the complex cellulose molecules in the wood into simpler organic compounds, returning nutrients to the soil. If it were plastic, the molecular breakdown would be much slower, potentially releasing microplastics into the environment, highlighting the importance of choosing biodegradable or easily recyclable materials.

Applying OCV to designing for disassembly: Objective: Design a mousetrap car that can be easily disassembled into its constituent materials at the end of its life. Constraint: The car must remain structurally sound during the race. Variable: Using mechanical fasteners (screws, clips) instead of permanent adhesives for attaching major components.

8. Summary of Key Concepts

Lifecycle: Every product has a life, from creation to end-of-life, and engineers must consider the entire journey. Recycling: Transforming old materials into new ones reduces waste and conserves resources. Reuse: Giving products or components a second life, either for their original purpose or a new one. Stewardship: Responsible design includes planning for a product's end-of-life, honoring God's creation.
