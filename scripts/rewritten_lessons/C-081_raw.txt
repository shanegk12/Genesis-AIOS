      "html": "<h2>Recycling, Upcycling, and Downcycling</h2><p>Not all material recovery is equal. There are three main paths a material can take when a product reaches the end of its life:</p><ul><li><strong>Recycling</strong> — The material is processed and turned into a new product of similar quality. Melting aluminum cans into new aluminum sheets is a classic example.</li><li><strong>Upcycling</strong> — The discarded material is creatively transformed into something of <em>higher</em> value. Old wooden pallets becoming designer furniture is a good example. The material gains value instead of losing it.</li><li><strong>Downcycling</strong> — The material is reused, but the process degrades its quality. Plastic bottles recycled into fleece fabric is downcycling — the plastic can't easily be turned back into bottles again. It's still better than a landfill, but it's a one-way street.</li></ul><p>When you design a product, understanding which path its materials will likely take at end of life helps you make better choices from the start. Upcycling is always the goal; downcycling is still better than discarding.</p>"

      "html": "<h2>From Macro to Micro: Why Materials Degrade</h2><p>When you look at a rusted car bumper or a crumbling concrete sidewalk, you're seeing a <strong>macro-level observation</strong> — something visible to the naked eye. But that rust and crumbling didn't appear out of nowhere. It started at the <strong>atomic level</strong>, where the bonds between atoms gradually broke down due to moisture, oxygen, stress, or temperature changes.</p><p>This is what <strong>multiscale modeling</strong> is all about: connecting what you can see with what's happening invisibly small. An engineer who only thinks about what a material looks like on the surface will miss the real story. Understanding atomic behavior helps you predict <em>when</em> a material will degrade, <em>how</em> to recover it most efficiently, and <em>which</em> materials are worth designing with in the first place.</p><h3>Common Signs of Material Degradation</h3><ul><li><strong>Corrosion</strong> — Rust on iron or steel; oxidation on copper turning it green.</li><li><strong>Cracking or fracturing</strong> — Stress fractures in concrete or brittle plastics.</li><li><strong>Delamination</strong> — Layers of composite materials separating over time.</li><li><strong>Discoloration or brittleness</strong> — UV exposure breaking down polymer chains in plastic.</li></ul><p>Each of these visible signs tells a story about what's happening at the molecular level — and each one affects whether the material can be recovered and how.</p>"

      "html": "<h2>Applying the OCV Method to Material Recovery</h2><p>You've already used the <strong>OCV method</strong> — Objective, Constraint, Variable — to frame engineering design problems. Material recovery is no different. Whenever you're designing a system to recover materials, or designing a product with end-of-life recovery in mind, OCV gives you a structured way to think it through.</p><h3>Objective</h3><p>What are you trying to achieve? Your objective might be to maximize the amount of aluminum recovered from a discarded appliance, or to design a product casing that can be cleanly separated and recycled at end of life.</p><h3>Constraint</h3><p>What limits you? Constraints in material recovery might include budget (recovery processes cost money), technology (some materials are harder to separate than others), safety (some materials are hazardous to handle), or time.</p><h3>Variable</h3><p>What can you change or adjust? Variables might include which materials you choose during design, how you bond or fasten parts together (screws vs. glue — one is much easier to disassemble), or what recovery process you specify.</p><p>A product that's been designed with disassembly in mind — sometimes called <em>design for disassembly</em> — is far easier to recover materials from. That's an optimization decision made long before the product reaches the end of its life.</p>"
